## Champagne and Bordeaux saw record-early first picking on 6 August, with Champagne's general harvest opening 12–17 August

- **headline** — The first 2026 grapes in Champagne and Bordeaux were picked on 6 August under special dispensation, a record-early first cut for both regions, with Champagne's general harvest opening 12–17 August.
- **detail** — In Champagne, grower Vincent Finot at Montgueux (Aube) obtained a derogation to pick Chardonnay on 6 August at over 11% potential alcohol, which Vitisphere reported as at least ten days ahead of the already-early 2025; in Bordeaux the same day, Merlot and Sémillon destined for sparkling wine were cut at around 9% potential alcohol with total acidity of 7–8 g/L. The general Champagne harvest then opened officially between 12 and 17 August depending on cru and variety, and Bordeaux's Grand Cru estates were reported as starting around 17 August against a normal first-or-second week of September.
- **date** — 2026-08-06 (first picking); official Champagne start 2026-08-12 to 2026-08-17
- **source_url** — https://www.vitisphere.com/actualite-107179-debut-des-vendanges-ce-6-aout-champagne-et-bordeaux-battent-un-nouveau-record-de-precocite.html
- **source_name** — Vitisphere
- **corroborated** — true — https://www.euronews.com/2026/08/20/champagne-harvest-why-picking-begins-as-early-as-mid-august (Euronews, 20 Aug 2026, confirms the 12–17 August official start and quotes Alexandre Gobillard of Gobillard & Fils: "On 15 August, go on holiday. Today it's 18 August, and we're in the middle of the harvest. We've never seen anything like it."). Also https://www.connexionfrance.com/news/heat-brings-early-bordeaux-wine-harvest-but-not-the-earliest-on-record/807690 (Connexion France, 13–14 Aug 2026) for the Bordeaux side.
- **confidence_note** — The "record" framing differs by region and by outlet. Vitisphere calls 6 August a new earliness record for both regions. Connexion France explicitly says Bordeaux's start is *not* an all-time record, citing 15 August 1893 as earlier — but that comparison is to the main red harvest, whereas the 6 August pick was sparkling-base fruit, so the two claims are about different things rather than contradictory. I could not verify the widely-repeated claim that spring frost destroyed 30–43% of Champagne's crop: it appears in search snippets from sources I could not fetch, so I have left it out. The CIVC's 8,800 kg/ha yield cap for 2026 was set on 23 July, before this window.
- **significance_notes** — This is the single biggest wine story of the window and the one every level of the trade is talking about, from négociants to sommeliers to consumers who read a general-news wire piece. Champagne and Bordeaux are the two most commercially and symbolically loaded appellations in the world, and an August harvest in Champagne is the kind of thing that gets picked up by Euronews and general dailies, not just the trade press. Within the trade it matters beyond novelty: an August pick changes labour scheduling, press capacity, acidity management and ultimately whether 2026 can be declared as a vintage Champagne year. Anyone in the wine business would know this happened.

## Burgundy's 2026 Crémant picking began 8 August, the earliest start the BIVB has on record, into a small crop

- **headline** — Burgundy began picking Crémant fruit on 8 August 2026, the earliest start in the BIVB's records, with growers reporting sharply reduced volumes.
- **detail** — The BIVB reported first Crémant de Bourgogne picking on 8 August 2026, a new earliness record beating the previous mark of 13 August 2022, with the first Chardonnays around 17 August and Pinot Noir shortly after; budburst had been around 28 March after a mild, wet winter. - **date** — 2026-08-08 (first picking); reporting 2026-08-24 and 2026-08-27
- **source_url** — https://www.bourgognes.wine/2026-harvest-in-burgundy/

# Knowledge decay in this domain

**Rough calibration: wine knowledge goes stale on a scale of months to years, not days — with one fast-moving exception.**

The domain's clock is the annual vintage cycle, and almost everything in wine is indexed to it. A harvest report is not a perishable news item; it is the opening entry in a permanent record about a vintage that will be sold, reviewed, cellared and re-tasted for the next thirty years. "Champagne and Burgundy picked in early August in 2026, with very low yields" is a fact that will still be true, still be repeated, and still be commercially relevant in 2036, because someone will be deciding whether to buy 2026 Champagne. Compare that to crypto, where a price level, a protocol upgrade or an exchange's solvency is obsolete within hours because the underlying object is continuously repriced by a market that never closes. Wine has no equivalent tick. The product literally cannot change faster than it ages.

Concretely, by component:

- **Harvest and vintage reports** — current for 6–12 months as *news*, then permanent as *reference*. The August report is the first draft; a fuller picture arrives at the end of harvest (October–November), and the real assessment only lands when the wines are tasted from barrel or bottle a year or more later. So a harvest claim decays into "early and incomplete but still directionally right" rather than into "wrong." An agent citing the 2026 Burgundy harvest start date in 2029 is still correct.
- **Critic scores and vintage charts** — the slowest-decaying content in the domain. A 2016 Barolo score published in 2020 is still actively used to price and sell that wine today. Scores get revised on re-tasting, but on a multi-year rhythm, and the original score never stops being quoted. Decay here is measured in years.
- **Appellation rules, AVA establishments, classifications** — near-permanent once made. INAO and TTB decisions are structural; Montpeyroux will be an AOC indefinitely. These entries barely decay at all, they only accumulate.
- **Company results, M&A, insolvencies** — decay on the ordinary business-news schedule: a set of annual results is the live number for about twelve months until the next set replaces it; an acquisition is permanent as a fact but stops being *news* in a few weeks. This is the fastest-moving genre in wine and it is still quarterly-to-annual, not daily.
- **Tariffs and trade rules — the exception.** This is the one part of the wine domain that can invalidate in days. Rates change by executive action, get struck down in court, get replaced by a different statutory mechanism, and get renegotiated; in this very research window I found reputable sources describing three different mechanisms (IEEPA, Section 122, Section 301) as governing US wine tariffs during 2026, and I could not reconcile them. A tariff claim more than a few weeks old should be treated as suspect. Health-guidance and regulatory-labelling stories sit somewhere in between — they change on a multi-year cycle but the *interpretation* shifts faster.
- **Market prices (Liv-ex indices, grape prices, land values)** — meaningful for weeks to a couple of months as a level, longer as a trend. Slower than equities because fine wine trades thinly and illiquidly.

So a decay-rate parameter for wine should be set an order of magnitude slower than for crypto or breaking politics — think a half-life of many months for most content, with a carve-out that makes trade-policy claims decay fast. The structural reason is that wine's news objects are physical goods with multi-decade lives produced on a once-a-year cycle, so the domain generates roughly one genuine information event per region per year, and the rest of the coverage is elaboration on it.
